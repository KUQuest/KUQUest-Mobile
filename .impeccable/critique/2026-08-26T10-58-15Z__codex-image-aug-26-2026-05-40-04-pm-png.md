## Design Specificity Verdict

### LLM assessment

The screens feel partially authored for KUQuest. The applicant reputation signals, Quest categories, team capacity, host identity, and application lifecycle are product-relevant details that a generic dashboard would not necessarily have. The green noticeboard direction also has a consistent visual signature.

The composition is still close to a category-interchangeable volunteer/job marketplace: dark green gradient hero, rounded white cards, pill tabs, stock-like activity imagery, progress bars, and outlined action buttons could be reused elsewhere with little change. The biggest loss of specificity is vocabulary: "mission," "mission quest," "My Apply Quest," and "MyQuest" do not form one coherent KUQuest model. The product context and existing design system use Quest, My Quests, Quest Application, and Review.

## Overall Impression

This is a striking, coherent visual concept with useful trust signals, but it currently reads as three attractive screens rather than one dependable app flow. The single biggest opportunity is to make the user's role and next decision explicit: what is a Quest, what is an application, where does selection happen, and how does a user move between root destinations? Once that is clear, reducing card density will make the polish work harder.

## What's Working

1. The green/warm-paper visual language is distinctive and consistent. Rounded surfaces, restrained shadows, and the dark header create a confident, welcoming tone without looking like a generic admin panel.
2. The cards expose the information people need to compare opportunities or candidates: title, category, date, location, host/profile, status, capacity/progress, and a next action.
3. Trust is made visible. Applicant photos, ratings, review counts, levels, skill tags, host identities, team counts, and lifecycle labels give users useful reasons to decide. The stepper in the first screen and the segmented states in the other two also provide orientation.

## Cognitive Load Assessment

Four of the eight checks fail across the set, with the highest load on the applicant-review screen:

- [x] Single focus: pass at the screen level; each screen has a recognizable job, although the first screen is weakened by competing actions.
- [ ] Chunking: fail. A single card carries roughly 8–10 distinct data points plus one or two actions, especially in My Apply Quest and MyQuest.
- [x] Grouping: pass. Cards, status areas, tabs, and summary panels are visually grouped clearly.
- [ ] Visual hierarchy: fail. The large hero/header and repeated action treatments compete with the data users need to compare.
- [ ] One thing at a time: fail. Check Apply asks users to sort, inspect profiles, message everyone, and select a team in one view; MyQuest cards offer edit, applicant, and detail paths together.
- [ ] Minimal choices: fail. The first screen exposes five candidates plus sort, five profile actions, Message All, and Select Team; this exceeds the four-item working-memory guideline at the selection point.
- [x] Working memory: pass. Key dates, locations, statuses, and progress are repeated in the relevant card rather than requiring users to remember another screen.
- [x] Progressive disclosure: pass. Profile/detail actions keep deeper information behind an explicit tap, even though the summary cards could still be shorter.

The visible decision point with more than four choices is the applicant list: five candidate rows and their profile actions appear alongside sorting and two footer actions.

## Emotional Journey

- Arrival: the dark green header, oversized title, and progress/tab state create a confident sense of momentum.
- Evaluation: photos, ratings, tags, host identity, status, and capacity create trust and make the data feel human. This is the strongest part of the experience.
- High-stakes moment: selecting a team or interpreting an application status is consequential, but the UI does not show selection state, selection count, or a clear confirmation path. That creates uncertainty exactly where reassurance matters most.
- End state: the green primary actions and the encouraging "Great job!" message provide warmth. The ending would be stronger if it confirmed the concrete outcome and next step rather than offering generic encouragement.

## Priority Issues

### [P1] Product language and role model drift

**What:** The first screen says "Check Apply" and "Who to work your mission quest"; the second says "My Apply Quest"; the third says "MyQuest" and "missions." These names blur the difference between a Quest, an application, and a host's created Quest.

**Why it matters:** A first-time user may not know whether they are applying, reviewing applicants, or managing a Quest they created. It also conflicts with the established KUQuest vocabulary and will make Thai/English localization harder.

**Fix:** Standardize on `Quest` everywhere. Use "Review Applicants" or "Review Applications" for the first screen, "My Applications" or "Applied Quests" for the second, and "My Quests" for the third. Make subtitles state the role: "Choose who can join your Quest," "Quests you've applied to," and "Quests you've created or joined."

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable clarify`

### [P1] Applicant selection is not discoverable or visibly guarded

**What:** In Check Apply, applicant cards only expose `View Profile`; there is no visible checkbox, selected state, selection count, or capacity rule. The footer's `Select Team` label suggests selection may happen later, while the stepper makes this screen sound like the review step. `Message All` has comparable visual prominence to the selection action.

**Why it matters:** The core host task—forming a team—has no visible interaction model. Users can review people without knowing how to choose, how many they can choose, or whether their choice was saved. Broad messaging also deserves deliberate confirmation.

**Fix:** If selection happens here, add a 48pt selection control and selected treatment to each row, show `0/3 selected` or equivalent capacity feedback, and keep the primary CTA disabled until valid. If selection is the next step, rename the CTA to "Continue to select team" and make the step boundary explicit. Demote `Message All` to a secondary/overflow action and confirm the recipient count before sending.

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable shape`

### [P1] Root navigation and screen hierarchy are ambiguous

**What:** My Apply Quest and MyQuest look like top-level destinations, but both show only a back arrow and help icon; the product's five-destination mobile navigation is not visible. The first screen is a multi-step flow, yet the same large header treatment makes it visually similar to a root screen.

**Why it matters:** A back arrow on a root screen creates uncertainty about where it goes, and users lose an obvious route to Quest Board, Chat, Create, or Profile. Flow context and app navigation need different chrome.

**Fix:** Keep persistent bottom navigation on root My Quests surfaces; reserve back navigation for the applicant-review flow and other child screens. Collapse or reduce the large hero after scroll while keeping the active tab/step context visible.

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable layout`

### [P1] Dense cards flatten comparison and action priority

**What:** Each My Apply Quest/MyQuest card combines a large image, category, title, date, location, host or description, status, application date, team progress, and one or two buttons. MyQuest's `Edit` and `View Applicants` are equal outlined buttons even though one is likely the primary next action for an active Quest.

**Why it matters:** Users must parse too much before comparing items, and equal buttons make the intended next step unclear. The page feels polished but slow to scan, especially one-handed.

**Fix:** Keep title + status + one key date + capacity/progress + one primary action in the list card. Move description/host details to the detail view, use one filled state-aware CTA, and place secondary actions such as Edit in an overflow menu or a less prominent text action.

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable distill`

### [P2] Accessibility and state semantics need verification

**What:** Pale green/amber status surfaces, small gray metadata, the green availability dot on applicant avatars, thin progress bars, and selected tab fills carry a lot of meaning. Some meaning is conveyed by color or subtle contrast alone, and the question-mark/help control is icon-only.

**Why it matters:** Low-vision users may miss status or availability; screen-reader users need the same context without the visual layout. Long Thai labels may also change the segmented tabs and button widths.

**Fix:** Validate text and status contrast on device, preserve text/icon labels for every state, add accessible labels and selected semantics, make the availability dot a labeled status, and test the longest Thai/English strings at small widths. Keep interactive hit areas at least 44–48pt.

**Suggested command:** `$impeccable audit`

## Persona Red Flags

### Jordan (first-timer)

- "Check Apply," "mission quest," and "My Apply Quest" are not natural task labels, so Jordan cannot confidently predict what happens next.
- `Level 12`, team progress, and the `?` icon have no visible explanation; Jordan must infer whether a level affects selection.
- On the applicant screen, Jordan sees `View Profile` but no selection affordance or count, so the primary host task is ambiguous.
- On the two My screens, the back arrow gives no clear route to the rest of the app.

### Casey (distracted mobile user)

- The large header/stepper consumes substantial vertical space before the actionable list, while the list cards require repeated scanning and scrolling.
- MyQuest presents two equal actions per card (`Edit` and `View Applicants`/`View Detail`), increasing thumb travel and decision time.
- The bottom actions on the first and third screens are useful for one-handed use, but the main root destinations are not persistently reachable in the screenshots.
- If the user is interrupted while reviewing or selecting applicants, the screenshots provide no visible saved-progress or resume cue; verify state persistence.

### Sam (accessibility-dependent user)

- Green avatar dots, progress fills, and tinted status chips may communicate availability/status visually without an equivalent text or announced state.
- The help question mark, pencil, chevrons, and some status icons need explicit accessibility labels and roles.
- Small gray date/location/metadata text and pale amber/green chips need real contrast testing, not visual approval from the mockup alone.
- The selected tab/step is strongly visual; expose selected/current semantics to VoiceOver/TalkBack and ensure long Thai labels do not clip.

## Minor Observations

- `5 / 8` should be labeled consistently as something like "5 spots filled of 8"; `Team Size` and `Team Progress` currently describe similar numbers differently.
- `10m ago` beside dates from May 2025 reads as internally inconsistent unless these are explicitly labeled demo fixtures.
- The three references use different artboard dimensions: 863×1823 for the first and 941×1672 for the other two. Establish one canonical device frame and safe-area grid before implementation.
- The status system is a good foundation: Under Review, Accepted, Waitlist, Declined, Open, Full, and Completed are easy to scan when text and icon remain present.
- Validate the same hierarchy in Thai; category labels, tab labels, and CTA strings will not have the same widths as English.
- The encouraging bottom messages are warm, but they should reflect the actual outcome (for example, "3 applicants selected") when the user completes a consequential action.

## Questions to Consider

- What if the first screen were called "Review Applicants" and showed the selection count persistently—would the host's decision become obvious without changing the visual language?
- Are these screens meant to be a single `My Quests` area with role-based tabs, or separate top-level destinations? The answer determines both the navigation chrome and the copy.
- What would remain in each card if the user had to compare ten Quests instead of three? That is the right test for the current information density.
